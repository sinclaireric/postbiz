Most content calendars fail the same way: you fill them in with enthusiasm on a Monday, and by the third week they’re empty. The cause is rarely a lack of ideas. It’s a pace that’s too ambitious, with no room for the unexpected.

## 1. Choose a pace you can keep

Start lower than you’d like. Three posts a week on one network, kept up for three months, beat two posts a day for a fortnight. You’ll raise the pace once it has settled.

## 2. Define recurring series

Series are recurring formats that save you from starting from a blank page. For example:

| Series | Example | Pace |
|---|---|---|
| Behind the scenes | A photo or an anecdote from the team | Once a week |
| Tip | A useful trick for your audience | Once a week |
| News | A new feature, an event | When there is some |
| Answer | A question you received, answered in public | Once every two weeks |

Three or four series are enough. Beyond that, the calendar becomes one more constraint.

## 3. Work in batches

Writing ten posts in one go is faster than writing one every morning: you stay in the same frame of mind, and you immediately see whether the posts look alike. Book a fixed slot, such as an hour on Thursday, to prepare the following week.

## 4. Keep a reserve

Always keep two or three “evergreen” posts ready: content that stays true on any day. They fill a gap when the week overflows, without hurting your consistency.

> [!TIP]
> Schedule the dated posts first (events, launches), then spread the series around them. The other way round forces you to move everything with each announcement.
